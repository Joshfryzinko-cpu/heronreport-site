Gulf County Commissioners Take Action on St. Joseph Bay — 2 Million Scallops on the Way

By John Watts, Editor | The Heron Report  |  October 27, 2026
St. Joseph Bay, Gulf County, Florida — the site of a first-of-its-kind scallop restoration project launched by county commissioners and FSU researchers. — Photo: The Heron Report

PORT ST. JOE — When red tide hit St. Joseph Bay and shut down the scallop season, some Gulf County residents decided to do something about it. And their commissioners listened.

At the October 14 Board of County Commissioners Special Meeting, commissioners unanimously approved a pilot scallop restoration project for St. Joseph Bay — the first of its kind in Florida. Commissioners Randy Pridgeon, Phil McCroan, and Spike McLemore were among those who championed the initiative and pushed it across the finish line.

"We take it seriously. It's an economic benefit to this community."

The project, driven in part by citizen advocate Jessica Graham and supported by researchers from Florida State University, involves deploying approximately 2 million scallop spats — juvenile scallops — into the bay. Local community members have already volunteered boats to help with the deployment. The county funded the effort at up to $20,000; it came in well under budget at $12,000, leaving money available for future restoration work.

Commissioner Pridgeon, who has been a consistent voice for bay health on the board, explained the urgency. Red tide in recent seasons damaged the scallop population and led regulators to close the bay to scallop harvesting. The loss hit local fishing families and the businesses that depend on healthy waters.

"We all know that this past year with scallops — last year we had a pretty good crop, but this past year we didn't because of the red tide," Pridgeon told the board. "It got some local citizens and people that work that bay a lot concerned, and they started making some calls."

Those calls turned into action. The FSU research group will bring the spats over and guide the deployment process, which requires precise timing — the spats must be released during a narrow window when they are ready to attach to seagrass. Researchers and volunteers will monitor the results, with the expectation that next season's bay should show measurable improvement.

Pridgeon also proposed documenting the project on video — capturing the spat deployment underwater — as both a community record and a tool for future grant applications. "Showing that we've got people doing this, that we take it seriously," he said. "It's an economic benefit to this community."

The board voted unanimously to support the project. For commissioners like McCroan and McLemore, who represent communities that have fished, boated, and built their livelihoods around St. Joseph Bay for generations, the investment is straightforward: healthy water is the foundation of Gulf County's economy and quality of life.

For residents watching the bay and its marine life, this is a step in the right direction — local leaders working with local people and university researchers to protect what makes this place worth living in.

The scallop spats are expected to be deployed in the coming weeks. The board will receive updates as the project progresses, with an eye toward expanding the effort and seeking grant funding to make it an annual restoration practice.
