Florida Passed Amendment 3. What Does It Mean for Gulf County?

By John Watts, Editor | The Heron Report  ·  November 4, 2026

PORT ST. JOE — Florida voters approved Amendment 3 on Tuesday, with about 62 percent voting yes, according to unofficial statewide results. For many Gulf County homeowners, the first savings will show up on their 2027 property tax bill. Gulf County projects it could lose about $3.56 million in property-tax revenue for its main operating fund. The county expects that loss to grow as the tax break gets bigger.

What changes on your tax bill?

A property tax bill has several lines. One goes to the school district. Others may go to the county, a city or a local fire district. Those other lines are what Amendment 3 calls non-school property taxes. Even if your town doesn’t have a school, your property can still be taxed by the school district it belongs to.

Example: You own and live in a home with an assessed value of $200,000 and qualify for the larger exemption.

2027: The county leaves the first $150,000 out of its tax calculation — leaving $50,000 to tax.

2028: The exemption rises to $250,000. If your home is still assessed at $200,000, the exemption covers the full value for non-school taxes. The school district still calculates its tax on $175,000 under the standard $25,000 school exemption.

People who become Florida residents after the end of 2026 generally qualify for the larger break in their fifth year of receiving a Florida homestead exemption. There is also a change for qualifying rentals, second homes and other properties without a homestead exemption. The yearly limit on how fast their assessed value can rise for non-school taxes drops from 10 percent to 5 percent. Local tax rates can still change.

Where would Gulf County feel it?

The county’s $3.56 million estimate is about 15 percent of the $23.8 million in property taxes it lists for its General Fund, which pays for much of the county’s day-to-day work. In its countywide property-tax breakdown for 2026–27, Gulf County says about 54 cents of every dollar goes to public safety, including the Sheriff’s Office, ambulance service, fire protection and 911 dispatch.

Some smaller fire districts could feel the loss more sharply. Gulf County estimates property-tax revenue could fall by about 40 percent in Tupelo, 37 percent in Howard Creek and 30 percent in Overstreet, compared with about 10 percent in the St. Joe Fire Control District. Before the election, officials also listed possible cuts involving parks, libraries and mosquito control. The county presented those cuts as options for discussion.

What about tourism money and other fees?

Gulf County charges a 5 percent lodging tax on qualifying short stays at hotels and vacation rentals. The county’s financial report puts that tax at about $4.93 million in fiscal 2025. The county also brings in money from building permits, ambulance bills, water service and waste disposal. Some of that money comes with rules on how it can be spent. A grant for a specific road project, for example, generally can’t be used to keep a library open longer.

The county-owned golf course offers a closer look at one spending decision. In fiscal 2025, it brought in about $1.25 million from operations and cost about $1.43 million to run, leaving it about $184,000 short before interest and transfers. The county moved about $1.12 million from its tourism fund to the course that year — $200,000 as a regular annual transfer and about $919,000 for expanding the clubhouse and pro shop.

How has Gulf County handled the money?

Independent auditors said Gulf County’s fiscal 2025 financial statements fairly showed its finances. They also reported that two issues raised the year before had been addressed. As of September 30, 2025, the county had about $10.1 million in its General Fund that had not been set aside for a specific purpose. Using some of that balance could help cover a shortfall for a time. The county would still have to figure out how to pay the bills in the years that follow.

The audit gives residents a look at how the county keeps its books. The next budget will show where commissioners choose to spend and where they choose to save. Before asking families to pay higher fees or get by with fewer services, commissioners should show where they looked for savings in county operations, how much those savings would cover and what each proposed cut would mean for the people who rely on that service.
