Gulf County Continues to Work With Scientists to Find Answers for St. Joseph Bay

Commissioners funded initial water testing and are seeking state and federal support for longer-term monitoring as scientists investigate what may be worsening the red tide bloom.

By Josh Zinko | The Heron Report  ·  November 25, 2026
A resident addresses Gulf County commissioners during Tuesday's meeting on St. Joseph Bay's red tide. — Photo: The Heron Report

PORT ST. JOE, Fla. — Gulf County commissioners returned to the question of what is happening in St. Joseph Bay on Tuesday, nearly a month after they paid for an initial round of water testing. With red tide still present in the region, the county is working with scientists to understand what might be worsening the bloom and seeking state and federal support to keep the investigation going.

Port St. Joe City Manager Michael Hammond told the board that he and District 5 Commissioner Phillip McCroan had discussed how unusual the bloom seemed for this time of year. Drawing on 52 years of local experience, Hammond said he could not recall seeing it persist this way into late fall.

The state's latest monitoring showed that the bloom remained active. In its Nov. 24 update, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission reported bloom-level concentrations of the red tide organism in 14 samples across Bay and Gulf counties over the preceding week.

For Commissioner Randy Pridgeon, the concern is also economic. After the board's Nov. 12 special meeting, Pridgeon told WJHG that even one bad weekend can hurt the people who make their living on the bay and affect a county that relies on visitors coming to its beaches.

From immediate testing to a longer-term plan

Commissioners voted on Oct. 27 to pay for an initial round of testing with the St. Andrew and St. Joseph Bays Estuary Program. Two days later, researchers collected water at six sites along the Gulf County Canal, the Intracoastal Waterway and the mouth of the canal. They tested for nutrients and bacteria to get a better picture of conditions in waterways that feed into the bay.

At that same October meeting, estuary program director Dr. Jessica Graham told commissioners she had submitted a RESTORE Act Pot 2 proposal to the Florida Department of Environmental Protection. The proposal would expand nutrient and bacteria testing, help researchers identify where bacterial contamination may be coming from and support a local water-quality lab. A nearby lab could shorten the wait for results and give researchers a better chance to follow up while conditions are still changing.

By Nov. 12, the county said commissioners were requesting state funding from DEP and working to secure federal support for the red tide response. At the special meeting that day, commissioners also approved letters seeking state help with water testing and fisheries-related relief. The county paid for the initial sampling. An award amount and timeline for the proposed expansion have yet to be confirmed.

What the water samples showed

The slides prepared for Tuesday's update show that researchers tested samples from three of the six sites for markers associated with human and cattle fecal waste. Neither marker was detected at those three sites. The testing covered a single round of sampling, leaving open whether results would differ elsewhere or after heavy rain.

The longer record presented to the board showed that nitrogen and phosphorus levels in the bay had generally held steady or declined in recent years, with measurements generally within the bay's numeric nutrient criteria. At the same time, chlorophyll-a, which scientists use to estimate the amount of algae in the water, had increased in some parts of the bay. Chlorophyll-a tracks algae broadly, so those trends leave open what is driving the increase and how much of it is connected to red tide.

With outside funding, researchers could sample the same areas repeatedly, compare conditions before and after rainfall and see whether problems recur in the same locations. That would give commissioners more evidence for deciding where to spend money on improvements and a way to measure whether those improvements are helping St. Joseph Bay.
