Gulf County Looks for Answers in St. Joseph Bay's Lingering Red Tide

City Manager Michael Hammond says this year's bloom is unlike anything in his decades of experience — and he wants it treated as the county's top priority.

By John Watts, Editor | The Heron Report  ·  November 24, 2026
A resident addresses Gulf County commissioners during Tuesday's meeting on St. Joseph Bay's red tide. — Photo: The Heron Report

PORT ST. JOE, Fla. — Port St. Joe City Manager Michael Hammond told Gulf County commissioners Tuesday that the timing of this year's red tide was troubling. Hammond said he and Commissioner Phillip McCroan had discussed the bloom and, drawing on more than 52 years of local experience, could not recall seeing red tide linger like this so late in the year.

Hammond's read on the situation carries weight precisely because of that experience. Few people in Gulf County government know St. Joseph Bay as well as he does, having spent decades working alongside the waterways, the fishing families and the businesses that depend on them. That depth of local knowledge is exactly why he is pushing the bloom to the front of the county's agenda.

"This needs to be our number one priority."
— Port St. Joe City Manager Michael Hammond, Nov. 24, 2026

Hammond told commissioners the bay's health underpins nearly everything else in the county, from fishing and tourism to the daily quality of life in Port St. Joe, and said the county can't afford to treat this bloom as business as usual.

Past blooms have lasted into the fall. Research based on state monitoring found elevated levels of Karenia brevis, the organism that causes Florida red tide, in St. Joseph Bay through late October 2005. In its Nov. 24 update, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission reported bloom-level concentrations in 14 samples across Bay and Gulf counties over the previous week.

A look at historical red tide events in St. Joseph Bay, tracking Karenia brevis cell counts by month and year. — Estuary Program data

The latest testing gave commissioners a partial answer about bacteria found during earlier sampling. The St. Andrew and St. Joseph Bays Estuary Program collected water Oct. 29 at six sites in and around the Gulf County Canal and Intracoastal Waterway. At three of those sites, lab tests looked for markers associated with human and cattle waste. Neither marker showed up. The canal was sampled on a single day, and the tests covered two possible sources, leaving the origin of the bacteria unresolved.

Years of monitoring across St. Joseph Bay turned up a different question. Total nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations have generally held steady or declined in recent years, but chlorophyll-a, a pigment scientists use to track algae in the water, has increased in parts of the bay. Most measurements were within the state's nutrient criteria, though the maps showed that some stations exceeded those benchmarks in individual years. At one southern bay station, nitrogen exceeded the benchmark in five years shown on the map.

One possibility raised in the presentation is that nutrients enter the bay in short bursts between sampling dates. Microscopic algae may also take up nutrients as they arrive, or the way nutrients are recycled within the bay may have changed. The data presented Tuesday leave open which explanation fits and which algae account for the rise in chlorophyll.

Getting those answers will take more sampling. Nitrogen and phosphorus results often come back three to nine months after collection, and nine of the 12 stations sampled monthly report totals without distinguishing forms such as nitrate or orthophosphate. The Estuary Program plans to trace nutrient sources across the bay and says it has U.S. Environmental Protection Agency funding to support five new stations that will measure nutrients, chlorophyll and basic water chemistry. The presentation gave no locations or start dates.

For Hammond, the open questions are reason enough to keep the pressure on. The Heron Report will continue to follow the county's response as more test results and the new monitoring stations come online.
